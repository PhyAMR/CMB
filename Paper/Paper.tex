\begin{abstract}
We investigate the statistical properties of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) angular correlation function $\xi(\theta)$ using Planck legacy data.
Three complementary statistics are studied:
the antipodal correlation $\xi_{180}$,
the interval-averaged correlation $\langle\xi\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$  between $\theta_a$ and $\theta_b$,
and the mean squared correlation $\langle\xi^2\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$.
These statistics are combined using the Aggregated Cauchy Association Test (ACAT) to quantify their overall significance, mitigating the `look-elsewhere' effect.
Our analysis shows that the observed CMB temperature is systematically less correlated than the predictions of the $\Lambda$CDM model at all---not only large---angular scales.
On the other hand, the statistical significance of the deviation is mild (one-sided p-value $p = ...$, equivalent to $\sim ... \sigma$), and therefore it can hardly be qualified as a cosmological tension.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{cosmic microwave background, angular correlation function, large-angle anomalies, Planck, $\Lambda$CDM}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec-intro}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Since the first measurements of the CMB temperature anisotropies, the angular two-point correlation function $\xi(\theta)$ has attracted particular attention.
At variance with the amplitudes $C_l$ of the power spectrum, this statistical descriptor highlights the largest angular scales, thus probing the longest wavelength perturbations accessible within the observable universe.

Early analyses of the COBE-DMR data already suggested that $\xi(\theta)$ was unexpectedly close to zero for separations larger than approximately $60^\circ$ \citep{Hinshaw_1996}.
Although the statistical power of COBE was limited, this feature was subsequently confirmed with much higher precision by the first-year WMAP observations \citep{Spergel_2003}, and the descriptor
\begin{equation}
S_{1/2} = \int_{-1}^{1/2} \xi^2(\theta)\,\dd(\cos\theta),
\end{equation}
was introduced to quantify the magnitude of the effect, measuring the integrated squared correlation function over angular separations larger than $60^\circ$ \citep{Spergel_2003}.
The observed value of $S_{1/2}$ was found to lie in the extreme tail of the $\Lambda$CDM distribution generated from Monte Carlo realisations, establishing the lack of large-angle correlations as one of the most persistent large-scale anomalies in the CMB.

A series of subsequent studies strengthened this conclusion based on successive WMAP data releases.
In particular, analyses of masked sky maps pointed out that the observed correlation function is nearly zero for $\theta \gtrsim 60^\circ$, with probabilities typically below $10^{-3}$ under the standard cosmological model \citep{Copi2006Uncorrelated, Copi2009, Copi2013Lack}.
These works emphasized that the discrepancy cannot be explained simply by the low quadrupole amplitude alone, but instead reflects an unusual covariance structure among the lowest multipoles.
They also noted connections with other large-scale anomalies, including the quadrupole-octopole alignment and hemispherical asymmetries.

On the contrary, \citet{Efstathiou_2010} argued that estimators based directly on masked-sky correlation functions can exaggerate the discrepancy with $\Lambda$CDM, and showed that full-sky maximum-likelihood reconstructions yield results more consistent with theoretical expectations.
Extending this argument, \citet{Pontzen2010} considered a broader class of anisotropic Gaussian models and concluded that the observed correlation function does not constitute compelling evidence for new physics once the full space of possible covariance structures is taken into account.
These studies highlighted the importance of estimator choice, a posteriori statistic selection, and the definition of the theoretical ensemble used when assessing significance.

The anomaly has also motivated the development of alternative statistical descriptors of large-scale correlations.
Direction-dependent generalizations of the correlation function were introduced to investigate whether the missing power is localized on the sky rather than uniformly distributed.
\citet{Zhang2012} constructed directional versions of both $\xi(\theta)$ and $S_{1/2}$, showing that a large fraction of the full-sky signal originates from regions associated with the quadrupole and octopole extrema.
Other approaches include bipolar harmonic decompositions \citep{Joshi2009Bipolar},
generalized isotropic angular correlation functions \citep{2023Capturing}, geometric representations of the two-point function \citep{Froes2015New}, and all-angle statistics such as
\begin{equation}
S_1 = \int_{-1}^{1} \xi^2(\theta)\,\dd(\cos\theta),
\end{equation}
proposed to reduce the dependence on the a posteriori choice of the $60^\circ$ threshold \citep{Gruppuso2014}.

Regarding the connection to other low-multipole features, \citet{Sarkar2010Missing} found essentially no correlation between the suppression of $\xi(\theta)$ and the well-known quadrupole-octopole alignments within $\Lambda$CDM realisations.
The simultaneous occurrence of both anomalies is therefore substantially rarer than each of them considered individually.
Similarly, \citet{Kim2011} argued that the observed odd-parity preference at low multipoles provides a phenomenological explanation for the deficit of large-angle correlations, although no convincing physical origin was identified.
Analyses of Planck polarization maps have revealed a similar suppression of large-angle correlations, with significances comparable to those found in temperature data \citep{Chiocchetta2020Lack-of-correlation}.
If confirmed, such results would suggest that the deviation from the predictions of the $\Lambda$CDM model is not solely a consequence of temperature-specific systematics and may instead reflect a more fundamental property of the largest observable scales.

Despite nearly two decades of study, there is still no consensus regarding neither the statistical significance nor the physical interpretation of the lack of temperature correlation.
In this work we revisit this issue based on Planck 2018 data.
In addition to the $S_{1/2}$ and $S_1$ statistics defined above, we also consider the antipodal correlation $\xi_{180}$, the average correlation $\langle\xi\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$ over 4 contiguous angular intervals, and the mean squared correlation $\langle\xi^2\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$ over the same intervals.
We construct random Monte Carlo realisations of the best-fit $\Lambda$CDM temperature spectrum to account for cosmic variance, and we combine the individual p-values of all statistics using the Aggregated Cauchy Association Test (ACAT) to obtain a global measure of the tension between the theoretical prediction and the observational measurements.

\subsection{Statistical descriptors}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

%\subsection{Angular Power Spectrum and Correlation Function}

It is customary to express the temperature anisotropies
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\Delta T(\hat{n})}{T_0} = \sum_{\ell,m} a_{\ell m} Y_{\ell m}(\hat{n})
\end{equation}
in terms of spherical harmonics $Y_{\ell m}(\hat{n})$ with real coefficients $a_{\ell m}$.
For a statistically isotropic Gaussian random field, the two-point statistics are
fully encoded in the amplitudes of the power spectrum $C_\ell = \langle |a_{\ell m}|^2 \rangle$ or, equivalently, the scaled spectrum $D_\ell = \ell(\ell+1)C_\ell/(2\pi)$, which is approximately flat for a scale-invariant spectrum of primordial fluctuations.
The angular two-point correlation function may then be obtained by transforming back to real space, yielding the polynomial expansion
\begin{equation}\label{eq-corr}
\xi(\theta) = \sum_\ell \frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi} C_\ell P_\ell(\cos\theta)
%= \sum_\ell \frac{2\ell+1}{2\ell(\ell+1)} D_\ell P_\ell(\cos\theta)
,
\end{equation}
where $P_\ell$ denote the Legendre polynomials.

%\subsection{Interval Statistics}

In addition to the descriptors $S_{1/2}$ and $S_1$ proposed in the literature, let us also consider the following statistical probes:

\textbf{Antipodal correlation}:
$\xi_{180} \equiv \xi(\theta = 180^{\circ})$ measures the correlation between antipodal points on the sky.
Because it is a pointwise evaluation of $\xi(\theta)$, it is relatively insensitive to masking effects at intermediate scales, and it provides a direct test of the alleged near-vanishing of large-angle correlations at the largest possible separation.

\textbf{Interval-averaged correlation}:
$\langle\xi\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$ corresponds to the mean of $\xi(\theta)$ over the angular interval $[\theta_a, \theta_b]$
\begin{equation}\label{eq-xiv}
\langle\xi\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b} = \frac{1}{\mu_a-\mu_b}\int_{\mu_b}^{\mu_a} \xi(\theta)\,d\mu 
\end{equation}
where $\mu_\theta \equiv \cos\theta$ accounts for the surface area of the sky as a function of polar separation angle, as in $S_{1/2}$ and $S_1$.
At variance with these traditional descriptors, $\xi_{180}$ and $\langle\xi\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$ preserve the information about the sign, distinguishing between correlated or anti-correlated temperature fluctuations.
Therefore, to partition the full range $0^\circ \le \theta \le 180^\circ$ into physically motivated regions, we select the null-crossings $\xi(\theta) = 0$ where the observed correlation function \citep{cosmoplanck_2020} changes sign, listed in Table~\ref{tbl-intervals}.

\textbf{Mean squared correlation}:
By analogy to $\langle\xi\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$ and $S_{1/2}$, we also evaluate the averge
\begin{equation}\label{eq-xi2}
\langle\xi^2\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b} = \frac{1}{\mu_a-\mu_b}\int_{\mu_b}^{\mu_a} \xi^2(\theta)\,d\mu 
\end{equation}
over the same annuli defined in Table~\ref{tbl-intervals}.
The quadratic statistic amplifies deviations that are coherent across multipoles.
It cannot be cancelled by sign changes of $\xi(\theta)$, and it is thus ideally suited to quantify (lack of) correlation within the interval.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For each statistic $k$, we compute the individual p-value $p_k$ as the one-sided empirical quantile of the observed value within the simulated ensemble: $p_k = F_k$, where $F_k$ is the
fraction of realisations where the prediction is less than or equal to the observed value.
The consistency between theory and data can be expressed in terms of Gaussian-like standard deviations as $n_\sigma = \Phi^{-1}(1 - p_\mathrm{global}/2)$, where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the probit function.

To obtain a calibrated global significance, the values of $\xi_{180}$, as well as $\langle\xi\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$ and $\langle\xi^2\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$ over the different intervals, must be combined together in a way that takes into account their correlation structure and the look-elsewhere effect.
Since they are all derived from the same realisation of $D_\ell$, their p-values are correlated by construction.
Reporting only the most extreme individual p-value would overestimate the statistical significance of any potential tension, while treating them as independent and applying a simple Bonferroni correction would be overly conservative.

As a compromise, we apply the Aggregated Cauchy Association Test \citep[ACAT;][]{Liu02012020}
\[
T = \sum_{k=1}^{11} w_k \tan\!\left[\left(\tfrac{1}{2} - p_k\right)\pi\right],
\]
with equal weights $w_k = 1/13$, reflecting no a priori preference for any particular angular interval or statistic.
Under the null hypothesis (that the observed value corresponds to a random realisation of the $\Lambda$CDM model), $T$ follows a standard Cauchy distribution regardless of the correlation among the $p_k$, and the combined p-value may be obtained analytically as
\[
p_\mathrm{global} = \tfrac{1}{2} - \frac{\arctan T}{\pi}.
\]
The Cauchy combination is exact under arbitrary dependence of the individual $p_k$, including the strong correlations expected between statistics computed over overlapping angular ranges.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec-discussion}

The results for alternative likelihoods and models with free curvature
$\Omega_k$ are reported in
Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-tables}. The dependence of
the results on the likelihood choice (CamSpec vs.\ Plik) provides a
useful cross-check, while the curvature-extended models test whether a
departure from flatness could alleviate the observed tensions.

As noted by \citep{Kim2011}, a lack of correlation is observed across
all angular scales---including large, intermediate, and small angles.
This is reflected in the fact that the observed values of
$\langle\xi^2\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$ fall below the distribution's median for all
intervals. This behavior is observed across all models and likelihoods
examined, as detailed in
Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-tables}. While the
significance of the individual statistics is not particularly high, the
consistent pattern of low $\langle\xi^2\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$ values across all
intervals suggests a coherent suppression of correlations that is not
confined to a flat model, but rather extends to models with free
curvature.

---

The statistics studied here are designed to probe complementary aspects of the large-angle correlation function: its integrated power, its mean level over specific angular ranges, and its value at antipodal separation.
The choice is guided by the existing literature on low-$\ell$ anomalies, though as noted by \citet{Efstathiou_2010}, some statistics, including $S_{1/2}$, were identified a posteriori from the data, which inflates their apparent significance.
Studying multiple statistics simultaneously, and combining their p-values through a global test, is therefore essential to obtain a reliable assessment of potential tensions with the $\Lambda$CDM scenario.

We note that defining the intervals from a feature of the data under
test is itself a form of a posteriori choice, of the kind discussed by
\citet{Efstathiou_2010} (Section~\ref{sec-intro}).

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec-conclusions}

We have studied the large-angle CMB angular correlation function and its
derived statistics using Planck 2018 data and two complementary
ensembles for the theoretical sampling distributions. The statistics
examined, the quadratic $\langle\xi^2\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$, the linear
$\langle\xi\rangle_{\theta_a}^{\theta_b}$, and the antipodal
$\xi_{180}$, probe distinct aspects of the large-angle temperature
field.

Both ensembles provide complementary perspectives on the significance of
the large-angle anomalies. The best-fit ensemble isolates the
contribution of cosmic variance, while the MCMC ensemble captures the
full parameter uncertainty and the cosmic variance.

The large-angle suppression of the correlation function, most evident in
$\xi_{180}$, is reproducible across both ensembles and across all
likelihood and curvature variants tested
(Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-tables}), but it is not
statistically significant once the eleven correlated statistics are
combined through the ACAT global test: the resulting tension with
$\Lambda$CDM is $\sim 1.1\sigma$ for the primary
\texttt{base\_CamSpecHM\_TT\_lowl\_lowE} model
(Table~\ref{tbl-camspec}).

We conclude that current Planck data do not provide significant evidence
against the standard cosmological model from the large-angle correlation
function.
